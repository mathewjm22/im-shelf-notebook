# Diastolic Murmurs

:::tip Shelf Tip
**Every diastolic murmur is pathologic.** There is no innocent diastolic murmur. The next step is always echocardiography.
:::

## What to Know

Diastolic murmurs occur between S2 and the next S1. On the internal medicine shelf, treat a diastolic murmur as pathologic until proven otherwise. The entire topic collapses into two buckets defined by **timing**:

- **Early diastolic decrescendo murmurs** = semilunar-valve regurgitation (AR, PR)
- **Mid-to-late diastolic rumbles** = atrioventricular-valve stenosis or increased flow (MS, TS)

The high-yield diagnoses are **aortic regurgitation (AR)**, **mitral stenosis (MS)**, and **pulmonic regurgitation (PR)**. Tricuspid stenosis is uncommon but can appear in questions testing inspiratory variation.

Think of diastole as the ventricular filling phase: a murmur means blood is either moving backward through an incompetent semilunar valve, or struggling to move forward through a narrowed atrioventricular valve.

### Why the shelf loves this topic

The exam reliably tests:
1. Localizing the lesion from murmur description + maneuver response
2. Recognizing the eponymous peripheral signs of chronic AR
3. Tying MS to rheumatic heart disease and its complications (AF, stroke, pulmonary hypertension, hemoptysis)
4. Distinguishing **chronic, compensated** valve disease (watchful waiting, medical therapy, elective surgery by echo thresholds) from **acute, catastrophic** regurgitation (endocarditis/dissection → surgical emergency)

### One-liner

**Early diastolic decrescendo = regurgitation (AR/PR); mid-diastolic rumble = stenosis (MS/TS). All diastolic murmurs are abnormal and earn an echo.**

## Most Commonly Tested

1. **Every diastolic murmur is pathologic — there is no innocent diastolic murmur — and the next step is always echocardiography.**
2. **Early decrescendo murmur at the left sternal border (3rd–4th ICS), loudest sitting up, leaning forward, in held expiration = AR; a mid-to-late low-pitched apical "rumble" = MS.**
3. **Wide pulse pressure (diastolic BP <60 mm Hg, SBP > 160 mm Hg) + bounding peripheral signs = chronic severe AR.** Low diastolic BP is itself an independent mortality marker in chronic AR.
4. **Acute severe AR (endocarditis or type A dissection) reverses the classic picture:** soft/short or absent murmur, narrow pulse pressure, NO cardiomegaly, with tachycardia and flash pulmonary edema/shock. It is a surgical emergency, not watchful waiting.
5. **Chronic severe AR gets surgery (AVR) when symptomatic (any LVEF), OR asymptomatic with LVEF ≤55%, OR asymptomatic with LVEF >55% but LVESD >50 mm (indexed >25 mm/m²)** — 2020 ACC/AHA.
6. **Severe MS = mitral valve area ≤1.5 cm²** (by 2D planimetry), with mean transvalvular gradient and pulmonary pressures gauging consequences — 2025 ESC/EACTS.
7. **A shorter A2–opening-snap interval means more severe MS** (<70 ms = severe). Higher LA pressure forces the valve open earlier.
8. **MS is rheumatic until proven otherwise.** Feared complications: atrial fibrillation, left atrial thrombus with embolic stroke, pulmonary hypertension, hemoptysis. New AF often "unmasks" previously silent MS.
9. **Handgrip and squatting (↑ afterload) INCREASE the AR murmur.** The MS/flow murmurs increase with exercise, leg raise, and left lateral decubitus with the bell. Standing/Valsalva (↓ preload) soften most murmurs.
10. **The Austin Flint murmur (apical diastolic rumble in severe AR) mimics MS but has NO opening snap and NO loud S1** — do not treat it as mitral stenosis.

## Physiology & Pathophysiology

The sound of a murmur is turbulent blood flow. During diastole, the aortic and pulmonic valves should be closed while the mitral and tricuspid valves should be open. Think about **where the pressure gradient is during diastole**, because a murmur is just turbulent flow driven by a gradient.

### Aortic regurgitation (early diastolic, decrescendo)

When the aortic valve fails to coapt, blood runs backward from the high-pressure aorta into the LV the instant the valve should close — right after S2. The gradient (aorta ≫ LV) is largest early in diastole and falls as the LV fills and aortic pressure drops, so the murmur is loudest at onset and **decrescendos**.

- **Chronic AR:** The LV dilates eccentrically to accommodate the regurgitant volume. This huge total stroke volume generates the **wide pulse pressure** and every bounding peripheral sign (Corrigan, de Musset, Quincke, etc.).
- **Acute AR:** The LV has had no time to dilate, so the regurgitant volume slams into a small, stiff ventricle → LVEDP skyrockets → flash pulmonary edema and a soft/short murmur with a *narrow* pulse pressure. The physiology flips, and so does the clinical picture.
- **Causes:** Leaflet disease (bicuspid valve, infective endocarditis, rheumatic disease) or aortic-root disease (aortic dissection, Marfan syndrome, aortitis).

### Pulmonic regurgitation (early diastolic)

Same mechanism on the right. In PR, blood returns from the pulmonary artery to the RV during diastole. Severe **pulmonary hypertension** raises pulmonary artery diastolic pressure and creates a high-pitched early diastolic decrescendo murmur called the **Graham Steell murmur**. Low-pressure PR (e.g., post–tetralogy of Fallot repair) is softer and lower-pitched because the regurgitant gradient is small.

### Mitral stenosis (mid-diastolic rumble + presystolic accentuation)

In MS, a narrowed mitral valve obstructs flow from the LA to the LV during diastole.

- **Mechanism:** Rheumatic inflammation causes leaflet thickening, commissural fusion, and subvalvular fibrosis. The fused-but-pliable leaflets snap open → **opening snap**. The still-mobile anterior leaflet closing forcefully gives a **loud S1**.
- **The rumble:** The low-pitched diastolic rumble is generated by turbulent transmitral flow. It is louder with exercise or tachycardia because faster flow across a fixed valve increases the gradient. This explains why **AF, fever, pregnancy, hyperthyroidism, and anemia can precipitate pulmonary edema in MS**.
- **Opening snap timing:** The classic opening snap occurs when a stenotic but still mobile mitral valve abruptly opens after S2. **A shorter A2-to-opening-snap interval means more severe MS** because elevated LA pressure forces the valve open earlier.
- **Presystolic accentuation:** Occurs when atrial contraction increases late-diastolic flow across the stenotic mitral valve. It **disappears in atrial fibrillation**.
- **Downstream complications:** LA pressure rises → LA enlargement → AF, LA thrombus, systemic embolism → pulmonary venous congestion → pulmonary hypertension → right heart failure. The dilated LA compresses the recurrent laryngeal nerve (**hoarseness, Ortner syndrome**) and ruptured bronchial veins cause **hemoptysis**.

### Functional diastolic rumbles

- **Austin Flint murmur:** Severe AR regurgitant jet strikes the anterior mitral leaflet, functionally narrowing the mitral valve and producing an apical mid-diastolic rumble. **There is no true mitral stenosis.**
- **Carey Coombs murmur:** Acute rheumatic fever can cause a mid-diastolic apical murmur due to valvulitis with increased flow across an inflamed mitral valve.
- **Other flow rumbles:** A short mid-diastolic rumble can occur with *normal* valves when volume across them is excessive — severe MR (increased mitral inflow), large VSD/PDA (across the MV), or ASD (across the TV).

## Clinical Presentation

Start by identifying timing, location, pitch, radiation, and response to maneuvers. **Have the patient sit forward at end-expiration for AR; use the bell in the left lateral decubitus position for MS.**

| Lesion | Classic murmur | Best heard | Associated findings |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Aortic regurgitation** | High-pitched, blowing, early diastolic decrescendo | Left sternal border, sitting forward, end-expiration | Wide pulse pressure, bounding pulses, displaced hyperdynamic PMI |
| **Mitral stenosis** | Low-pitched mid-diastolic rumble with opening snap | Apex, left lateral decubitus, bell | Loud S1, AF, hemoptysis, malar flush, pulmonary hypertension |
| **Pulmonic regurgitation** | High-pitched early diastolic decrescendo | Left upper sternal border | Loud P2 and signs of pulmonary hypertension |
| **Tricuspid stenosis** | Mid-diastolic rumble | Left lower sternal border | JVD, hepatomegaly, ascites; increases with inspiration |

### Chronic AR vignette patterns

- Young patient with **bicuspid aortic valve** or **connective-tissue disease (Marfan)**
- Fever, new AR murmur, embolic phenomena, or IV drug use → **infective endocarditis**
- Sudden severe tearing chest/back pain, pulse deficits, neurologic symptoms, or unequal arm BPs with a new AR murmur → **acute aortic dissection**
- Chronic severe AR: exertional dyspnea, orthopnea, palpitations, angina, or eventually heart failure
- Vital sign signature: **wide pulse pressure** with high systolic and low diastolic BP (e.g., 150/55). Low diastolic BP is itself an independent mortality marker.
- **Peripheral buzzwords (all from large stroke volume + rapid runoff):**
  - **Corrigan/water-hammer pulse** (bounding, collapsing)
  - **de Musset sign** (head bobbing)
  - **Quincke pulse** (nail-bed capillary pulsations)
  - **Traube sign** ("pistol-shot" femorals)
  - **Duroziez sign** (femoral to-and-fro bruit)
  - **Müller sign** (uvula pulsation)
  - **Hill sign** (popliteal SBP ≫ brachial)
  - **Pulsus bisferiens** (two systolic peaks)

### Acute AR

Looks nothing like chronic AR — abrupt **cardiogenic shock / pulmonary edema, tachycardia, hypotension, narrow pulse pressure**, and a *soft, short* murmur. **Context is the tell:**
- Fever + new murmur → endocarditis
- Tearing chest pain radiating to the back → dissection

The reversal of the classic exam is the single most tested "atypical" trap on this topic.

### MS vignette patterns

- Classically a **young-to-middle-aged woman** (~80% female), often an **immigrant from a region where rheumatic fever remains prevalent**, often with remote childhood rheumatic fever
- Progressive dyspnea, orthopnea, fatigue, **hemoptysis**, or palpitations due to AF
- **Pregnancy or a new tachyarrhythmia can unmask symptomatic MS** because reduced diastolic filling time markedly raises the transmitral gradient
- **Hoarseness** from LA enlargement compressing the left recurrent laryngeal nerve = **Ortner syndrome**
- Exam: loud S1, opening snap after S2, low-pitched diastolic rumble at the apex (left lateral decubitus with the bell), presystolic accentuation (lost once AF sets in)
- **Malar flush ("mitral facies")** and signs of pulmonary hypertension (loud P2, RV heave)
- Severity-by-exam buzzword: **shorter S2–opening-snap interval (≈60–70 ms) = more severe MS**

### Atypical presentations worth knowing

- **Elderly AR:** Classic wide-pulse-pressure signs may be masked by concomitant hypertension and stiff, noncompliant arteries. Mild AR is common (~2% over age 70) and usually non-progressive.
- **Degenerative (calcific) MS in the elderly:** Increasingly replaces rheumatic MS in high-income countries. Driven by **mitral annular calcification (MAC)**, seen in older patients with HTN, diabetes, CKD, COPD. Presents with a higher valve area relative to gradient than rheumatic MS, and lacks the pliable "hockey-stick" leaflet / loud S1 + opening snap of rheumatic disease (leaflets are rigid/calcified).
- **MS "silent" until stressed:** Patients can be asymptomatic at rest and decompensate only with the tachycardia/volume load of pregnancy or new AF.

## Diagnosis

**The initial test for a suspected pathologic diastolic murmur is transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) with Doppler.** It identifies valve anatomy, severity, chamber remodeling, ventricular function, and pulmonary pressures. Diastolic murmurs are highly specific for structural valve disease, but do not rely on auscultation alone to grade severity.

### Severity criteria — the "echo labs" that matter

| Parameter | Severe AR | Severe MS |
|---|---|---|
| **Vena contracta width** | >6.0 mm (≥0.6 cm) | — |
| **Jet width / LVOT** | ≥65% | — |
| **Regurgitant volume / fraction** | RVol ≥60 mL/beat; RF ≥50% | — |
| **EROA (effective regurgitant orifice area)** | ≥0.3 cm² | — |
| **Pressure half-time** | <200 ms supports severe (>500 ms suggests mild) | — |
| **Aortic diastolic flow reversal** | Holodiastolic, end-diastolic velocity ≥20 cm/s in descending/abdominal aorta | — |
| **Mitral valve area (MVA)** | — | **≤1.5 cm² = severe (≤1.0 cm² = very severe)** |
| **Mean transvalvular gradient** | — | ≥5–10 mm Hg (WHF requires ≥4 mm Hg for rheumatic Dx) |

### Workup by lesion

**Aortic regurgitation:**
- TTE evaluates valve morphology, aortic-root dimensions, regurgitant severity, LV size, and LVEF
- **For AR staging:** LVEF and LV end-systolic dimension drive surgical timing (severe asymptomatic C2 = LVEF <55% or LVESD >50 mm)
- Obtain **TEE** if endocarditis, prosthetic-valve dysfunction, or dissection-related AR is suspected and TTE is inadequate
- Obtain **CT angiography urgently** when acute aortic dissection is suspected
- ECG may show LV hypertrophy in chronic severe AR; CXR may show cardiomegaly or pulmonary edema

**Mitral stenosis:**
- TTE assesses MVA, transmitral gradient, valve morphology, LA size, pulmonary pressures, and concomitant MR
- **Severe MS:** MVA ≤1.5 cm². The mean transmitral gradient is flow dependent, so interpret it in the context of heart rate and cardiac output.
- **TEE is important** before percutaneous mitral balloon commissurotomy or cardioversion when AF is present — evaluates LA appendage thrombus and valve morphology
- **Wilkins (echo) score ≤8** predicts favorable anatomy for balloon valvotomy
- ECG may show AF or LA enlargement (**"P mitrale"** — broad, notched P wave)
- CXR: LA enlargement (straightened left heart border, double density, splayed carina), cephalization, pulmonary edema, Kerley B lines

### Imaging buzzwords

**MS on echo:**
- **"Hockey-stick" / doming of the anterior mitral leaflet in diastole**, with **restricted leaflet-tip mobility and commissural fusion** but a pliable leaflet body = rheumatic MS

**AR on imaging:**
- Color Doppler: regurgitant jet filling ≥65% of the LVOT
- **Holodiastolic flow reversal in the descending/abdominal aorta** = specific severity sign
- Catheterization buzzwords: **pulsus bisferiens**, exaggerated peripheral systolic amplification, and in acute AR, **LVEDP approximating aortic end-diastolic pressure** with loss of the dicrotic notch
- CXR: cardiomegaly from chronic LV dilation (absent in acute AR)

### Key bedside distinction

An apical diastolic rumble **plus opening snap and loud S1** suggests true MS. An apical rumble in a patient with signs of severe AR may be an **Austin Flint murmur** — look for AR findings (wide pulse pressure, bounding pulses, early diastolic decrescendo murmur) and the **absence** of opening snap/loud S1.

### Demographic clues NBME rewards

- **Rheumatic MS** → young-to-middle-aged woman (~80% female), often an immigrant from an endemic region. High-prevalence regions present in teens–age 30 with pliable valves; low-prevalence regions present at 50–70 with calcified valves decades after rheumatic fever.
- **Degenerative/calcific MS** → older patient (~70 years) with HTN, diabetes, CKD, COPD (MAC)
- **Chronic AR** → bicuspid aortic valve (≈¼ of significant AR, younger patient with aortic root dilation), prior endocarditis, aortic root disease (Marfan), or degenerative calcific disease in the elderly
- **Acute AR** → think the acute precipitants: infective endocarditis (IVDU, fever, new murmur) or type A aortic dissection (tearing chest/back pain)

### Rule out mimics

- Dyspnea and pulmonary edema differential: HFpEF, COPD/asthma, PE, pneumonia, anemia, thyrotoxicosis
- Rumble + hemoptysis + AF vignette: confirm rheumatic MS and exclude atrial myxoma (positional "tumor plop," constitutional symptoms)

## Management

**There is no single treatment for "a diastolic murmur."** Acute management targets the underlying lesion and any hemodynamic instability.

### Acute severe aortic regurgitation

:::danger Surgical emergency
Acute severe AR from endocarditis, aortic dissection, prosthetic-valve failure, or trauma is a **surgical emergency**. Recognize this and call surgery immediately — valve replacement/repair should not be delayed, especially with hypotension, pulmonary edema, or low output.
:::

**Step-by-step:**

1. **Recognize** this as a surgical emergency when caused by aortic dissection, infective endocarditis, prosthetic-valve failure, or trauma
2. **Stabilize** airway and oxygenation; treat pulmonary edema with positive-pressure ventilation and diuresis as clinically indicated
3. **Afterload reduction with IV nitroprusside** when BP permits — improves forward stroke volume
4. **Inotrope** such as dobutamine or dopamine for low-output states to augment forward flow and lower LVEDP
5. **Avoid interventions that prolong diastole** — especially beta-blockers (slower HR increases regurgitant time). *Exception: aortic dissection, where beta-blockade is used but given cautiously.*
6. **Avoid intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP)** — diastolic balloon inflation increases aortic diastolic pressure and worsens AR
7. **Obtain urgent cardiothoracic surgery and cardiology consultation.** Acute AR due to dissection requires urgent surgical management; manage the dissection pathway concurrently.

### Acute decompensated mitral stenosis

**This is a heart-rate problem** — tachycardia shortens diastole and the filling time across the stenotic valve, spiking LA pressure.

1. **Treat pulmonary edema** with oxygen/positive-pressure support and IV loop diuretics
2. **Correct the trigger:** AF with rapid ventricular response, fever, anemia, infection, pregnancy-related volume change, or thyrotoxicosis
3. **Slow the rate** with a **beta-blocker** (or non-dihydropyridine CCB) to lengthen diastolic filling time
4. In **AF with RVR**, use rate control. If unstable, perform synchronized cardioversion.
5. **Anticoagulate** when indicated for AF, prior embolism, or documented LA thrombus
6. **Definitive intervention:** percutaneous mitral balloon commissurotomy for symptomatic severe rheumatic MS with favorable anatomy. Surgery when anatomy is unfavorable, significant MR is present, or another cardiac surgery indication exists.

:::warning Shelf trap
In acute pulmonary edema from severe MS, **do not reflexively give large fluid boluses.** The fundamental problem is elevated LA pressure behind an obstructed mitral valve.
:::

### Chronic AR

- Follow asymptomatic patients with **serial TTE** based on severity and LV dimensions
- Treat hypertension (SBP >140 mm Hg) with **afterload-reducing agents**: ACE inhibitor, ARB, or dihydropyridine CCB. For symptomatic severe AR or LV dysfunction in patients with prohibitive surgical risk, use GDMT (ACE inhibitor/ARB and/or sacubitril-valsartan).
- **Medical therapy does not fix the valve.** Surgery (AVR or valve-sparing root replacement) is the only definitive treatment.
- **Surgical indications (2020 ACC/AHA):**
  - Symptomatic severe AR → surgery regardless of LVEF
  - Asymptomatic severe AR with **LVEF ≤55%**
  - Asymptomatic severe AR with LVEF >55% but **LVESD >50 mm** (or indexed >25 mm/m²)
- Address the cause: aortic surveillance in bicuspid aortic valve/aortopathy, treatment of endocarditis, evaluation for inflammatory aortic disease when relevant

### Chronic MS

- **Prevent recurrent rheumatic fever** with secondary antibiotic prophylaxis (long-acting penicillin) when rheumatic heart disease is the cause
- **Manage AF** with rate control and anticoagulation when indicated. **Rheumatic MS + AF = firm indication for warfarin** (not DOACs); particularly high thromboembolic risk.
- Encourage prompt evaluation of worsening symptoms (exercise tolerance, palpitations, orthopnea, hemoptysis, edema)
- **Definitive management is mechanical relief of obstruction:**
  - **Percutaneous mitral balloon commissurotomy (PMBC/PMV)** = first-line for symptomatic severe MS (MVA ≤1.5 cm²) with favorable anatomy (**Wilkins ≤8**), no LA thrombus, and less than moderate MR
  - **Surgical commissurotomy or valve replacement** when PMBC is contraindicated or anatomy is unfavorable
- **PMBC contraindications: ≥moderate MR or LA thrombus**
- Medical therapy improves symptoms but does not remove the fixed obstruction

### Endocarditis prophylaxis

Routine prophylactic antibiotics are **not** recommended solely for native valvular lesions. Use prophylaxis only for selected high-risk cardiac conditions undergoing specified dental procedures.

### Special populations

**Pregnancy:**
- **MS is particularly dangerous in pregnancy** because plasma volume and heart rate increase, raising the transmitral gradient ~50%, often precipitating heart failure in the **second trimester**. HF occurs in ~⅓ of pregnant women with MVA <1.5 cm².
- Manage with **careful diuresis and rate control** (beta-blockade). Percutaneous balloon mitral commissurotomy can be considered for severe symptomatic MS refractory to medical therapy, typically **after 20 weeks**.
- Severe AR is often **better tolerated** than severe MS because systemic vascular resistance falls during pregnancy — but acute AR remains an emergency.
- ACE inhibitors contraindicated in pregnancy.

**Older adults:**
- **Calcific mitral annular disease** can cause degenerative MS. These patients often have comorbid diastolic dysfunction, CKD, and AF, making gradient interpretation more complex.
- Chronic AR in older adults requires careful assessment of LV function, coronary disease, aortic dimensions, and operative risk.

**Immunocompromised or people who inject drugs:**
- High index of suspicion for **infective endocarditis** when a new AR murmur occurs with fever, bacteremia, embolic findings, or heart failure. Acute AR due to IE may deteriorate rapidly.

**Pediatric overlap:**
- In children, a diastolic murmur is pathologic and warrants cardiology evaluation. Congenital causes include bicuspid aortic valve, repaired congenital heart disease, and pulmonic-valve abnormalities.

## Common Test Questions

### Vignette 1 — Diagnosis

> A 34-year-old man is evaluated for exertional fatigue. BP is 150/55 mm Hg. On exam his carotid pulses are bounding and collapse quickly, his head bobs with each heartbeat, and a high-pitched, blowing decrescendo murmur is heard at the left sternal border, loudest when he sits up and leans forward in held expiration. What is the most appropriate next step?

**Answer:** Transthoracic echocardiography to confirm and grade **chronic aortic regurgitation**.

**Why:** Wide pulse pressure (150/55), Corrigan pulse, de Musset sign, and a leaning-forward decrescendo murmur are textbook chronic AR; TTE establishes etiology, severity, and LV size/EF. Wrong answers: "reassurance" ignores that diastolic murmurs are always pathologic; "beta-blocker" doesn't treat AR and can paradoxically raise SBP by increasing stroke volume; "coronary angiography" skips the diagnostic echo.

### Vignette 2 — Acute management

> A 58-year-old man develops sudden severe dyspnea and hypotension. He has fever and blood cultures positive for *Staphylococcus aureus*. Examination reveals pulmonary crackles and a new early diastolic decrescendo murmur along the left sternal border. Echocardiography shows severe AR with a mobile aortic-valve vegetation. What is the most appropriate next step?

**Answer:** **Urgent surgical evaluation for aortic valve intervention** while treating infective endocarditis, with IV **nitroprusside** for temporary afterload reduction.

**Why:** Acute severe AR from endocarditis causes abrupt LV volume overload, pulmonary edema, and shock. Antibiotics are necessary but may not be sufficient; acute heart failure due to valve destruction is a surgical indication. Wrong answers: **IABP is contraindicated**; **IV metoprolol** removes the compensatory tachycardia and can precipitate collapse; "IV antibiotics and observe" is inadequate for hemodynamically significant acute AR.

### Vignette 3 — Mechanism / Mimic

> A 67-year-old man has exertional dyspnea and palpitations. Examination shows a wide pulse pressure, bounding peripheral pulses, a high-pitched early diastolic murmur at the left sternal border, and a low-pitched apical mid-diastolic rumble without an opening snap. Which mechanism best explains the apical murmur?

**Answer:** Functional obstruction of mitral inflow by an aortic regurgitant jet (**Austin Flint murmur**).

**Why:** Severe AR can direct a regurgitant jet against the anterior mitral leaflet, causing functional mitral narrowing and a diastolic rumble. True MS would be more likely to have an opening snap, loud S1, rheumatic valve morphology, and marked LA enlargement.

### Vignette 4 — Special population

> A 29-year-old woman at 26 weeks' gestation, who emigrated from a region with endemic rheumatic fever, develops progressive dyspnea and orthopnea. Exam reveals a loud S1, an opening snap, and a low-pitched apical diastolic rumble; HR is 118/min. Echo shows a mitral valve area of 1.2 cm². What is the best initial therapy?

**Answer:** A **beta-1–selective beta-blocker** (to lengthen diastolic filling), with **diuretics** for congestion.

**Why:** Severe rheumatic MS decompensating in the second/third trimester; tachycardia shortens diastolic filling and raises LA pressure, so rate control is first-line, with diuretics for pulmonary congestion. If symptoms or pulmonary pressures persist despite medical therapy, **percutaneous balloon commissurotomy after 20 weeks** is next. Wrong answers: cardiopulmonary-bypass surgery carries high fetal loss and is reserved for life-threatening failure; ACE inhibitors are contraindicated in pregnancy; "immediate C-section" is not the initial step.

### Vignette 5 — Diagnosis + complication

> A 42-year-old man presents with progressive exertional dyspnea and palpitations. He emigrated from an area where rheumatic fever is common. Cardiac examination reveals an irregularly irregular rhythm, a loud S1, an opening snap shortly after S2, and a low-pitched diastolic murmur at the apex. Which diagnosis is most likely?

**Answer:** Mitral stenosis with atrial fibrillation.

**Why:** The opening snap and apical diastolic rumble are classic for MS. LA enlargement from chronic MS predisposes to AF. AR produces an early diastolic decrescendo murmur rather than an opening snap. MR is holosystolic and radiates to the axilla.

## Key Evidence & Guidelines

- **2020 ACC/AHA Guideline for Valvular Heart Disease** (published 2021) — Chronic severe AR surgery if symptomatic (any LVEF), LVEF ≤55%, or LVESD >50 mm / indexed >25 mm/m². Severe MS = MVA ≤1.5 cm². Integrated echocardiographic evaluation and timely intervention for severe symptomatic valve disease or ventricular decompensation.
- **2025 ESC/EACTS Valvular Heart Disease Guidelines** — Rheumatic MS intervention limited to clinically severe MS (MVA <1.5 cm²) with suitable anatomy.
- **ACC/AHA vs. ESC/EACTS Guideline Comparison** (JACC 2023) — Concordant on surgery for symptomatic severe AR and asymptomatic AR with LVESD >50 mm / indexed >25 mm/m².
- **American Society of Echocardiography — Native Valvular Regurgitation Recommendations** (2017) — Integrated approach using vena contracta, regurgitant volume/fraction, EROA, flow reversal, ventricular response, and Doppler findings to grade AR severity.
- **ASE Rheumatic Heart Disease Guidance** (2023) — Echocardiographic assessment of rheumatic MS anatomy, hemodynamics, pulmonary pressures, and pregnancy-related risk.
- **2025 ESC Guidelines on Cardiovascular Disease and Pregnancy** — MS management (beta-blockers/diuretics, PMC after 20 weeks); maternal risk markers (NYHA >II, PAP >30 mm Hg, severe stenosis).
- **ACC/AHA/ACCP/HRS Atrial Fibrillation Guideline** (2023) — Anticoagulation and rhythm/rate management, including AF associated with valvular heart disease.
- **ACC/AHA Aortic Disease Guideline** (2022) — Surveillance and management of bicuspid aortic valve-associated aortopathy and thoracic aortic disease.
- **Rheumatic Heart Disease JACC Focus Seminar** (2023) — RHD as the dominant global cause of MS and surgical considerations.

## Pearls & Pitfalls

### Buzzwords and classic associations

- **Blowing early decrescendo murmur, leaning forward** → aortic regurgitation
- **Water-hammer/Corrigan pulse, head bobbing, nail-bed pulsations, wide pulse pressure** → chronic severe AR
- **Austin Flint murmur (apical rumble, no opening snap)** → severe AR mimicking MS
- **Soft/short diastolic murmur + flash pulmonary edema + narrow pulse pressure** → acute AR (think endocarditis or dissection)
- **Opening snap + loud S1 + apical rumble + left lateral decubitus/bell** → mitral stenosis
- **Malar (mitral) flush, hemoptysis, AF, young immigrant woman** → rheumatic MS
- **Hoarseness + large left atrium** → Ortner syndrome (RLN compression from MS)
- **High-pitched decrescendo diastolic murmur + pulmonary hypertension** → Graham Steell murmur (PR)
- **Diastolic murmur after tetralogy of Fallot repair** → low-pressure pulmonic regurgitation
- **ECG "P mitrale" (notched, broad P wave)** → left atrial enlargement from MS
- **"Hockey-stick" doming anterior mitral leaflet on echo** → rheumatic MS
- **Short A2-opening-snap interval** → severe mitral stenosis
- **Diastolic rumble that increases with inspiration** → tricuspid stenosis
- **Fever + new AR murmur + positive blood cultures** → infective endocarditis with acute AR
- **Tearing chest pain + new AR murmur + pulse deficit** → acute aortic dissection

### Common wrong answers and traps

1. **Calling an early diastolic decrescendo murmur "aortic stenosis."** AS is a *systolic* ejection murmur. Early diastolic decrescendo points to regurgitation — usually AR or PR.

2. **Starting a beta-blocker or placing an IABP in acute severe AR.** The trap works because beta-blockers and IABP are reflexive "heart stabilizers." In acute AR the patient is preload/rate-dependent; beta-blockade removes the compensatory tachycardia, and diastolic IABP inflation augments backward flow — both can kill. Correct: **afterload reduction (nitroprusside) + emergent surgery**.

3. **Assuming all apical diastolic rumbles are mitral stenosis.** Severe AR can produce an **Austin Flint murmur** — no opening snap and no loud S1. Look for AR findings (wide pulse pressure, bounding pulses, early diastolic decrescendo murmur) and treat the AR, not the mitral valve.

4. **Using the opening snap as a marker of mild MS.** The opposite is true: a **shorter** interval from A2 to the opening snap reflects higher LA pressure and **more severe** MS.

5. **Medically "treating" the valve in chronic AR to avoid surgery.** Vasodilators control hypertension and symptoms but do *not* alter disease course or defer the need for AVR once echo thresholds (symptoms, LVEF ≤55%, LVESD >50 mm) are met. Picking "continue medical therapy" when a surgical trigger is present is wrong.

6. **Treating symptomatic severe MS with diuretics alone.** Diuretics reduce congestion but do not correct the obstructive lesion. Suitable patients need balloon commissurotomy or surgery.

7. **Choosing balloon valvotomy for MS with left atrial thrombus or ≥moderate MR.** Both are contraindications; the answer is surgery (and anticoagulation/thrombus resolution first).

8. **Forgetting anticoagulation in rheumatic MS with new AF.** Rheumatic MS + AF is a classic firm indication for **warfarin** (not DOACs); selecting "no anticoagulation / aspirin only" is a trap.

9. **Mislabeling murmur maneuvers.** Students reflexively say "inspiration increases all murmurs." Only **right-sided** murmurs (TS, PR, TR) reliably increase with inspiration; **handgrip** augments the **left-sided** AR and MS murmurs.

10. **Giving large fluid boluses in decompensated MS.** The problem is already elevated LA pressure. More fluid worsens pulmonary edema.
