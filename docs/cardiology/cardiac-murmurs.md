# Cardiac Murmurs

:::tip Shelf Tip
Timing first. Location second. Maneuvers third. If you can decide whether a murmur is **systolic, diastolic, or continuous**, you've already narrowed the differential more than most of your classmates will.
:::

## What to Know

Murmurs are easiest to compare by **shape first**, then by **where they're loudest**, and finally by **how they behave with maneuvers**.

- **Shape:** Listen for a "rev up and then down" (crescendo-decrescendo, usually an ejection murmur like AS) vs. a constant "rev" throughout (holosystolic, usually a regurgitant lesion like MR). Where that rev peaks tells you how severe the lesion is.
- **Location:** Use the **MTAPS** anatomy below to narrow which valve.
- **Maneuvers:** Change preload and afterload at the bedside, then watch what the murmur does. This separates HCM from AS from MVP from MR.

Then layer on the classic seven features: **timing, shape, location, radiation, intensity, pitch, and quality**.

### Big-picture categories

- **Ejection systolic murmurs** point toward outflow lesions or a benign flow murmur.
- **Holosystolic murmurs** point toward regurgitant or septal lesions (physiologic, pregnancy).
- **Diastolic murmurs** = structural valve disease until proven otherwise.
- **Continuous murmurs** = present through both systole and diastole (classic example: PDA).

### One-liners worth memorizing

- **Aortic stenosis** → ejection (crescendo-decrescendo) systolic murmur
- **Mitral regurgitation and tricuspid regurgitation** → holosystolic murmurs
- **VSD** → usually holosystolic
- **HCM** → ejection murmur that becomes more notable with standing (opposite of AS)
- **Innocent flow murmur** → soft and position-dependent

## Most Commonly Tested

- Differentiating **AS vs. HCM vs. MVP vs. MR** using bedside maneuvers (classic shelf vignette)
- Identifying holosystolic murmurs: **MR, TR, VSD**
- Recognizing diastolic murmurs as **pathologic until proven otherwise**
- Where to listen for each murmur (MTAPS acronym)
- Why **inspiration louder = right-sided**, **expiration louder = left-sided**
- Innocent vs. pathologic systolic murmur features

## Physiology & Pathophysiology

### MTAPS: where to listen

Walk through the chest from the patient's right upper sternal border down and across. This is also where you can remind yourself of the MTAPS acronym.

| Area | Location | Systolic murmurs | Diastolic murmurs |
|---|---|---|---|
| **M**itral | 5th ICS, midclavicular line | MR, MVP | MS |
| **T**ricuspid | Left lower sternal border | TR, VSD | TS, ASD |
| **A**ortic | Right 2nd ICS | AS, aortic sclerosis, flow murmur | AR |
| **P**ulmonic | Left 2nd ICS | Flow murmur, pulmonic stenosis | Pulmonic regurgitation |
| **S** (HCM) | Along left sternal border | HOCM | — |

### Inspiration vs. expiration (right vs. left)

- **Right-sided murmurs** (TR, pulmonic lesions) get **louder with inspiration** (increased venous return to the right heart) and softer with expiration.
- **Left-sided murmurs** generally get **louder with expiration**.

In one bedside study, murmurs from the right-sided chambers were best separated from others by **"augmentation of their intensity with inspiration"** and **"diminution of their intensity with expiration."** That's the actual language worth having in your head — it maps directly to how questions get written.

### Diastolic = pathologic until proven otherwise

A diastolic murmur usually means structural valve disease and generally warrants echocardiography or referral rather than watchful reassurance.

**Mnemonic — reverse MTAPS for diastolic:** **M**itral and **T**ricuspid *stenosis*; **A**ortic and **P**ulmonic *regurgitation*.

## Diagnosis

### Step-by-step evaluation (sub-page: Step by Step Evaluation)

Decide **when in the cardiac cycle** the murmur occurs, then use location, radiation, intensity, pitch, quality, and maneuvers to narrow the cause.

1. **Start with timing.** If you can decide whether the sound is systolic, diastolic, or continuous, you've already narrowed the differential a lot.
   - If the rhythm is fast or irregular, **palpating the carotid upstroke can help separate S1 from S2** (remember: S1 is the first part, S2 is the second part).

2. **Describe the murmur with the classic seven features:** timing, shape, location, radiation, intensity, pitch, and quality.
   - **Timing:** Again — if it's in the first part then it's systolic; if it's in the second part or the second beat then it's diastolic. If it's throughout the entire thing then possibly PDA. If it's just the systolic but the whole thing, then think VSD.
   - **Shape:** This is that crescendo-descendo of aortic stenosis basically.
   - **Location:** Use the MTAPS anatomy above.

3. **Treat diastolic murmurs as pathologic until proven otherwise.** Why? A diastolic murmur usually means structural valve disease and generally warrants echo or referral rather than watchful reassurance.

4. **For systolic murmurs, use the pattern.**
   - **Ejection systolic murmurs** point toward aortic stenosis, pulmonic stenosis, HCM, or a benign flow murmur.
     - Think benign in patients like pregnancy, kiddos
     - Think HOCM in your young athlete → always get an echo; remember the maneuvers to differentiate from MVP
     - AS and PS can be differentiated from the location where you auscultate (see MTAPS)
   - **Holosystolic murmurs** raise MR, TR, or VSD.
   - A pediatric review also notes that **innocent murmurs are usually systolic, soft, and often louder when supine**, whereas **pathologic murmurs are more likely to be holosystolic, diastolic, grade 3 or louder, harsh, associated with an abnormal S2, or louder with standing.**

5. **Use bedside maneuvers to sort the major lesions.** The most useful learning point to pass on is that *active auscultation matters more than memorizing names*. Changes with standing, squatting, inspiration, and handgrip can help separate flow murmurs, aortic stenosis, mitral regurgitation, and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. (See full maneuvers table under Management.)

6. **Don't ignore the rest of the exam.** History and associated findings matter: symptoms, family history, abnormal pulses, blood pressure differences, cyanosis, heart failure signs, or a click/opening snap all increase the chance that the murmur is structural rather than innocent.

7. **A reality check on exam accuracy.** In asymptomatic subjects, one prospective study found the physical exam was fairly accurate for valvular disease, with about **70% sensitivity and 98% specificity** — but that still means some important lesions are missed. Every part of the question stem is there for a reason.

### Benign vs. pathologic systolic murmurs (quick comparison)

| Favors innocent | Favors pathologic |
|---|---|
| Grade ≤ 2 intensity | Grade ≥ 3, harsh |
| Louder when supine | Louder with standing |
| Position-dependent, soft | Associated with abnormal S2 or click |
| No associated findings | Thrill, abnormal pulses, HF signs |

Those features push you away from a simple innocent murmur.

### One-liner summary

Aortic stenosis is an ejection murmur; mitral regurgitation and tricuspid regurgitation are holosystolic murmurs; VSD is usually holosystolic; HCM is an ejection murmur that becomes more notable with standing; and an innocent flow murmur is soft and position-dependent.

## Management

### Systolic murmurs — the bedside game (sub-page: Systolic)

For systolic murmurs, the bedside game is to **change preload and afterload, then watch what the murmur does.**

- **Aortic stenosis** is the one where maneuvers help mostly by *exclusion*.
- **Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy** is the classic preload-sensitive murmur. It gets **louder with Valsalva and when standing**, and it gets **softer with squatting, passive leg raise, and handgrip**.
- **Mitral regurgitation and VSD** behave like left-sided regurgitant murmurs: **handgrip and transient arterial occlusion make them louder**, while **amyl nitrite makes them softer**.
- **Right-sided murmurs**, especially tricuspid regurgitation and pulmonic flow lesions, get **louder with inspiration and softer with expiration**. In the same bedside study, murmurs from the right-sided chambers were best separated from others by *"augmentation of their intensity with inspiration" and "diminution of their intensity with expiration."*
- An **innocent flow murmur** is usually softer and position-dependent. The pediatric review says innocent murmurs are *"systolic, less than grade 3 in intensity, and louder when the patient moves from an upright position to a supine position."* That pattern fits increased flow rather than structural obstruction.

### Maneuvers — the core shelf table

Valsalva/standing make HCM louder; handgrip makes MR and VSD louder; inspiration makes right-sided murmurs louder; squatting tends to blunt HCM and can help bring out some other systolic ejection murmurs; a soft murmur that gets louder supine is more likely innocent. **None of these maneuvers is 100% accurate.**

| Maneuver | Physiologic effect | HOCM | AS | MVP | MR |
|---|---|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| **Valsalva / Standing** | ↓ Blood return to LV | ↑ | ↓ | ↑ | ↓ |
| **Laying down / Squatting / Straight leg raise** | ↑ Blood return to LV | ↓ | ↑ | ↓ | ↑ |
| **Hand isogrip / Phenylephrine** | ↑ Afterload | ↓ | ↓ | ↓ | ↑ |
| **Amyl nitrite** | ↓ Afterload | ↑ | ↑ | ↑ | ↓ |
| **Post-PVC** | ↓ Afterload + ↑ Volume (↑ Diastolic time) | ↑ | ↑ | ↓ | ↓ |

*Source: REBEL EM*

### How to reason through it — not just memorize (sub-page: Maneuvers and Mitral Valve Prolapse)

**HOCM and MVP are "low-volume lovers"** — meaning they like low volume; that is when the murmurs sound louder. They are the opposite of mitral stenosis, mitral regurgitation, aortic stenosis, and aortic regurgitation. Those usual players like more blood against the diseased valve.

**Standing and Valsalva (↑ MVP murmur):**
- Standing and Valsalva are *low-volume maneuvers* that decrease venous return/preload.
- With low volumes and MVP, the valve will fall back down on itself, increasing the sound of the murmur.

**Squatting and leg raise (↓ MVP murmur):**
- Squatting and leg raise are *high-volume maneuvers* that increase venous return/preload.
- When blood is put back in the left ventricle, the redundant valve cannot fall back down on itself. So squatting is a high-volume maneuver that puts blood back into the left ventricle, and it is not going to make the murmur of MVP sound louder because the valve is not going to fall in on itself.

**Sustained hand grip (↑ MVP murmur):**
- Sustained hand grip increases afterload (imagine hand grip as someone sitting on the aortic valve).
- With hand grip, there is more pressure resisting blood flow from the left ventricle and out through the aortic valve. Since increasing afterload will prevent blood from flowing in a normal forward path, this increases preload and will increase any murmurs that are due to backward-flowing blood (like mitral regurgitation and MVP).
- With MVP and handgrip, you increase left ventricular pressure — and what happens to the mid-systolic click? **It is still there, and if anything is louder.** Because what did you do? You increased the pressure in your left ventricle.

### Diastolic murmur approach

Treat as pathologic until proven otherwise. Get an echo.

### Continuous murmur approach

Continuous = present through systole AND diastole. Classic shelf example: **PDA**.

## Common Test Questions

### Vignette 1

> A 17-year-old athlete is evaluated for a murmur noted at a preseason physical. The murmur is heard best at the left lower sternal border. **It becomes louder when he stands up from a squatting position and softer when he squats.** What is the most likely diagnosis?

**Answer:** Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HOCM)

**Why:** Standing ↓ preload → less blood in the LV → the hypertrophied septum obstructs more → murmur louder. Squatting does the opposite. This pattern distinguishes HOCM from AS (which gets *softer* with standing). Always get an echo in a young athlete with this murmur.

### Vignette 2

> A 72-year-old woman has a crescendo-decrescendo systolic murmur best heard at the right upper sternal border, radiating to the carotids. She has a slow-rising, delayed carotid pulse (pulsus parvus et tardus). Her murmur becomes softer with sustained handgrip. What is the diagnosis?

**Answer:** Aortic stenosis

**Why:** Location (right 2nd ICS), radiation to carotids, shape (crescendo-decrescendo), and parvus et tardus pulse all point to AS. Handgrip ↑ afterload → less forward flow across the aortic valve → softer murmur.

### Vignette 3

> A 28-year-old pregnant woman is noted to have a soft, grade 2 early systolic murmur at the left upper sternal border. It is louder when supine and softer when standing. No radiation, no click, normal S2. What should you do next?

**Answer:** Reassurance — this is a benign flow murmur of pregnancy.

**Why:** Pregnancy increases cardiac output and causes benign flow murmurs. The features (soft, position-dependent, louder supine, no associated findings) are classic for an innocent murmur per the pediatric review criteria. No echo needed.

## Pearls & Pitfalls

- **"HCM and MVP are low-volume lovers"** — remember this and the whole maneuvers table makes sense.
- Aortic stenosis murmur is **ejection** (crescendo-decrescendo) and radiates to the carotids.
- MR and TR are **holosystolic**; VSD is usually holosystolic.
- HCM gets **louder with standing** — this is the single most useful shelf discriminator from AS.
- Diastolic murmurs = pathologic until proven otherwise.
- Innocent flow murmur = **soft, systolic, grade < 3, position-dependent (louder supine)**.
- Right-sided murmurs louder with **inspiration** ("augmentation with inspiration"); left-sided with expiration.
- A **mid-systolic click** that *persists or gets louder* with handgrip points to MVP — because handgrip increases LV pressure.
- When rhythm is fast or irregular, **palpate the carotid upstroke** to separate S1 from S2.
- Pathologic systolic murmur clues: harsh, loud, abnormal S2 or click, or louder with standing rather than supine.
- Every detail in a shelf question stem is there for a reason — physical exam is only ~70% sensitive, so the writers include specific features to narrow your answer.
- No maneuver is 100% accurate; you're building a probabilistic picture, not making a definitive diagnosis.
