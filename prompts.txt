# OpenEvidence Prompt Pack — Shelf Exam Content

**Important:** OpenEvidence works best with focused clinical queries, not long content-generation requests. Instead of one mega-prompt, this is a **sequence of 5 focused prompts** you run back-to-back for each condition. Each prompt is scoped to what OpenEvidence does best (evidence synthesis with citations). You paste all 5 responses together into Claude at the end.

Total time per condition: ~5 minutes in OpenEvidence, then paste to Claude.

---

## How to use

1. Open OpenEvidence (grant it any search permissions it asks for)
2. Replace `[CONDITION]` in each prompt with your topic (e.g., "Diabetic Ketoacidosis," "Community-Acquired Pneumonia")
3. Run each prompt as a **separate query** in OpenEvidence (don't paste all 5 at once — OpenEvidence won't handle that well)
4. Copy each response as it comes
5. When you've run all 5, paste them to Claude with:
   > "Create a new page for [CONDITION]. Here are the OpenEvidence responses: [paste all 5 clearly separated]"

   Or, if you also have OneNote content:
   > "Create a page for [CONDITION]. Here's OpenEvidence output: [paste 5 responses]. Here's my OneNote content: [paste or attach]. Merge both — prioritize my teaching voice but use OpenEvidence for evidence and current numbers."

## The 5 Prompts

### Prompt 1 — Diagnosis & Pathophysiology

```
I'm teaching an internal medicine shelf exam review on [CONDITION]. Provide a focused summary with inline citations covering:

1. Pathophysiology — the key mechanistic cascade a medical student must understand, in 1-2 paragraphs
2. Diagnostic criteria — the formal criteria, scoring systems (if any), and specific numerical thresholds that establish the diagnosis
3. Required workup — labs, imaging, and tests needed to confirm the diagnosis and rule out key mimics
4. Common differential diagnoses that get mistaken for this condition on the shelf exam

Use bullet points and tables where helpful. Cite guidelines and primary sources with year of publication.
```

### Prompt 2 — Clinical Presentation & Buzzwords

```
For [CONDITION] on the internal medicine shelf exam, provide:

1. Classic clinical presentation (history, symptoms, physical exam findings) — including vital sign patterns and classic buzzword phrases that shelf question writers use
2. Atypical presentations worth knowing (e.g., silent presentations in diabetics or elderly, if applicable)
3. Lab patterns that are pathognomonic or highly suggestive — give specific values when they matter
4. Imaging findings with buzzword descriptions (e.g., "ground-glass opacities," "pulsus parvus et tardus")
5. Demographic clues (age, sex, risk factors) that point toward this diagnosis in a vignette

Format as a clear list that a student can scan quickly. Cite sources where specific numbers or findings come from a guideline or primary study.
```

### Prompt 3 — Management (Acute & Chronic)

```
For [CONDITION] on the internal medicine shelf, give me the management approach with inline citations:

1. First-line acute/initial management — specific drugs, doses when they matter, fluid choices, order of operations
2. Common wrong-answer traps on shelf exam management questions (e.g., "giving insulin before fluids in DKA") — explain WHY the trap works and what the correct reasoning is
3. Chronic/outpatient management — long-term treatment, prevention of recurrence, discharge criteria
4. Special populations (pregnancy, elderly, renal/hepatic dysfunction, immunocompromised) — only if there are meaningful differences
5. When to escalate (ICU admission criteria, surgical referral, specialist consult)

Cite current guidelines (year specified) for key recommendations.
```

### Prompt 4 — Key Trials & Evidence

```
Summarize the 3-5 most important landmark trials, major guidelines, or evidence sources relevant to [CONDITION] for an internal medicine learner. For each, give:

- Trial name or guideline name + year
- One-sentence summary of what it established
- Why it matters clinically / what practice it changed

Keep it concise — these are the evidence anchors a student should be able to name on rounds, not a full literature review.
```

### Prompt 5 — High-Yield Shelf Points

```
Give me the 10 highest-yield facts about [CONDITION] that the NBME internal medicine shelf exam reliably tests. These should be the "if a student only has 15 minutes to study this topic, learn these" points. Be specific — not "know the management" but "know that bicarbonate is NOT routinely given in DKA unless pH < 6.9."

Also give me 5 classic buzzword/finding → diagnosis associations for this condition. Format as:
- "Finding/phrase → what it suggests"

Cite guidelines where the recommendation comes from a specific source.
```

## Why 5 prompts, not 1

Research on OpenEvidence shows it was designed for **focused point-of-care questions** — the sweet spot is specific clinical queries that get synthesized evidence back with citations. Giving it a 500-word mega-prompt with 13 required sub-sections tends to produce:
- Shorter, more generic answers per section
- Lost citations
- Sections that get dropped or merged

Breaking into 5 focused prompts means each query gets OpenEvidence's full attention and best citation output. Yes, it's a few more clicks — but the output quality is much higher.

## If you want to be lazy

For a quick single-prompt alternative that will produce something usable (just less rich), use this:

```
I'm teaching an internal medicine shelf exam review on [CONDITION]. Produce a comprehensive teaching summary with inline citations covering: pathophysiology, diagnostic criteria with specific thresholds, classic clinical presentation and buzzwords, first-line acute management with specific drugs and doses, chronic management, and the 10 highest-yield points the NBME shelf exam tests on this topic. Flag common wrong-answer traps and explain why they're wrong. Cite current guidelines by year.
```

Expect a shorter, less detailed response — but it's still a good scaffold. Paste it to Claude the same way.

---

## Pro tips

**Use medical abbreviations and specifics.** Per OpenEvidence's own guide, "In a patient with T1DM, with the following labs: pH 7.15..." produces better output than "Tell me about DKA." The 5-prompt format already builds this in, but if you want to go deeper on any single aspect, add specifics.

**Follow-up prompts are free.** If a section is weak, run a follow-up: "Expand on management of [CONDITION] in pregnancy with specific drug choices and safety data." OpenEvidence maintains context within a session.

**Save high-performing prompts.** OpenEvidence has a "slash prompt" feature (type `.` in the ask bar) to save and reuse custom prompts. You could save each of the 5 above as `.shelf1`, `.shelf2`, etc. and run them with one keystroke.

**Trust but verify specific numbers.** OpenEvidence has very low citation fabrication rates (per a 2026 study auditing ~5,000 citations, essentially none were fabricated) — but drug doses, criteria thresholds, and guideline-year specifics are still worth double-checking against UpToDate or the guideline itself before putting them in front of students.
